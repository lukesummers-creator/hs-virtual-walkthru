## 1) The estimator’s workflow (the shortest path to accurate “days”)

### Step A — Measure consistently (or your rates won’t mean anything)

Pick one consistent takeoff method and never change it—because surface area ↔ labor & materials only becomes usable when measurement is consistent.

**Minimum takeoff you need:**

- Walls: sq ft
    
- Ceilings: sq ft
    
- Trim: linear ft (base + casing + crown if included)
    
- Doors: count (and whether painting **1 side or 2 sides**, slab vs 6-panel, etc.)
    
- Windows: count (only if you’re painting sash/grids/frames)
    
- Closets: count (closets often add lots of cut-in time)
    

### Step B — Decide the “mode”: Low / Mid / High

Many professional estimating systems explicitly assume different productivity tiers (“Slow / Medium / Fast”) depending on conditions and crew capability.  
HOMEstretch translation:

- **Low estimate = Slow mode** (fussy work, lots of masking, heavy prep, occupied, detail-heavy)
    
- **Mid estimate = Medium mode** (typical listing repaint, normal prep, decent access)
    
- **High estimate = Fast mode** (vacant, open walls, minimal trim detail, repeatable rooms)
    

### Step C — Convert takeoff → hours using production rates

Industry best practice is to track rates in the units you estimate in: **sqft/hr**, **lnft/hr**, **hrs/door**, etc.

### Step D — Convert hours → painter-days

Use **productive hours per painter-day**, not 8.0.  
Field rule of thumb that matches reality:

- **Occupied home:** 5.5–6.0 productive hrs/day (lots of protection, moving stuff, interruptions)
    
- **Mostly vacant:** 6.0–6.5 productive hrs/day
    
- **Vacant + easy access:** 6.5–7.0 productive hrs/day
    

Then:

> **Painter-days = Total labor hours ÷ productive hrs/day**  
> **Calendar days = Painter-days ÷ painters on crew**

## 2) The big idea: separate “field rolling” from “edge/detail time”

Walls/ceilings look simple, but time is mostly driven by:

- **Edges (cut-in)** + **masking/protection** + **prep**
    
- Not the rolling itself
    

So estimate walls as two buckets:

1. **Field application (rolling/spraying the open area)**
    
2. **Edges/detail (cut-in, tight areas, around trim, corners, outlets, returns)**
    

This is the single biggest “instinct upgrade.”

## 3) Baseline production rates (low / mid / high)

These are **training baselines** you can use today, then refine with your own job logs.

### A) Walls — brush/cut + roll (most common interior method)

Use these as **ALL-IN wall rates** for “cut + roll” (includes cut-in + rolling, not heavy prep).

**Walls (cut + roll), per coat, per painter**

- **Low (slow):** 40–60 sqft/hr
    
- **Mid:** 60–90 sqft/hr
    
- **High (fast):** 90–120 sqft/hr
    

Why these ranges are reasonable:

- Published contractor examples show wall “cut & roll” rates can be quite slow when you include real-world edges/texture/two-coat logic.
    
- Other estimator guidance emphasizes tracking your own rates because conditions swing massively job-to-job.
    

**Important nuance:** “roller-only open wall” can be much faster (hundreds of sqft/hr), but that’s not the total wall system when you include cut-in and masking.

### B) Ceilings — roll (typical)

Ceilings are slower because:

- overhead fatigue,
    
- more splatter control,
    
- lighting reveals lap marks,
    
- often more stain-spot priming.
    

**Ceilings (roll), per coat, per painter**

- **Low:** 50–70 sqft/hr
    
- **Mid:** 70–100 sqft/hr
    
- **High:** 100–130 sqft/hr
    

### C) Trim (base + casing + crown) — brush/mini-roller combo

Trim speed depends hugely on profile detail + tape strategy + whether you’re painting both sides of casing returns.

**Trim, per coat, per painter**

- **Low:** 40–60 lnft/hr
    
- **Mid:** 60–90 lnft/hr
    
- **High:** 90–120 lnft/hr
    

A published example range shows detailed trim can drop dramatically versus “easy straight run.”

### D) Doors — by “hours per door” (this is where people miss big)

Doors are deceptively slow because of:

- panel grooves,
    
- sanding/deglossing,
    
- hardware removal,
    
- dry time logistics,
    
- rehang and touchups.
    

**Interior door (paint), per door, per coat system**  
Assume: light prep + 2 finish coats (no major stripping)

- **1 side only (in place):**
    
    - Low: 1.0–1.5 hrs/door
        
    - Mid: 0.75–1.0 hrs/door
        
    - High: 0.5–0.75 hrs/door
        
- **Both sides (remove/lay flat, then reinstall):**
    
    - Low: 2.5–3.5 hrs/door
        
    - Mid: 2.0–2.5 hrs/door
        
    - High: 1.5–2.0 hrs/door
        

Field discussion examples often land in this general neighborhood once prep + multiple applications + rehanging are included.

## 4) The criteria checklist (the “why did this take longer?” encyclopedia)

### 4.1 Protection & masking complexity (often the #1 time swing)

**Add time when:**

- Occupied home (furniture, rugs, decor, kids’ rooms)
    
- Lots of glass/floor transitions
    
- Fresh flooring or refinished hardwood (extra care)
    
- Built-ins / bookcases / shelving in place
    

**Rule of thumb for protection overhead (per room):**

- Vacant: +0.25–0.5 hr
    
- Lightly furnished: +0.5–1.0 hr
    
- Fully furnished / tight: +1.0–2.0 hr
    

### 4.2 Prep level (this is where your low/mid/high should diverge)

Use a simple “prep grade” and apply a multiplier:

- **Prep A (light):** scuff, minor nail holes  
    Multiplier: **1.0x**
    
- **Prep B (medium):** multiple patches, caulk runs, light sanding throughout  
    Multiplier: **1.15–1.30x**
    
- **Prep C (heavy):** cracking/peeling, water stains, texture repairs, significant sanding/deglossing  
    Multiplier: **1.40–1.80x**
    

(Also: stains often introduce **spot prime + dry time + second spot**, which crushes flow.)

### 4.3 Accessibility / height

Add time for:

- 9–12' ceilings (ladder work, slower cut)
    
- Stairwells, 2-story foyers
    
- Tight closets
    
- Rooms full of obstacles
    

Typical multiplier:

- Normal 8': **1.0x**
    
- 9–10': **1.1–1.2x**
    
- 11–12' and/or stairwell: **1.25–1.5x**
    

### 4.4 Color & sheen changes (coverage risk)

Add time when:

- Dark → light, red/yellow coverage risk
    
- Switching sheens (flat → eggshell; satin → semi on trim)
    
- Painting over glossy/oil without proper bonding steps
    

Typical adders:

- **Extra coat risk:** +50–100% of wall/trim paint time for that surface
    
- **Degloss/bonding step:** +0.25–0.75 hr per room (light) or more (heavy glossy)
    

### 4.5 Application method (speed vs prep tradeoff)

Spraying is faster _on open surfaces_, but can be slower overall if masking is intense or ventilation constraints exist. Many sources highlight the prep/cleanup tradeoff for spraying.

**Practical rule:**

- **Brush/roll wins** in occupied homes with moderate detail.
    
- **Spray wins** in vacant homes, cabinet/trim packages (if masked well), or when a factory finish is required—but only if your crew is set up for it.
    

### 4.6 Disassembly / reassembly (where estimates silently die)

Don’t “hope it fits.” Decide scope explicitly:

- Remove & reinstall switch plates? (Usually yes)
    
- Remove door knobs/hinges? (Often yes if painting doors)
    
- Remove blinds/curtain rods? (Sometimes)
    
- Remove HVAC registers? (Sometimes)
    

If you want a simple training adder:

- **Per switch plate/outlet cover:** +1–2 minutes each (remove/replace)
    
- **Per door knob/strike plate set:** +5–10 minutes per door
    
- **Door removal + rehanging:** +15–30 minutes per door (depends on hinges/hardware/labeling)
    

(These adders are exactly why door packages blow up timelines.)

## 5) Putting it together: a repeatable estimating formula

### Walls example formula

**Wall hours (per coat system)**

1. Compute wall area (sqft)
    
2. Choose rate (sqft/hr) based on low/mid/high
    
3. Multiply by coats
    
4. Multiply by multipliers (prep, access, occupied)
    

> **Wall hours = (Wall sqft ÷ rate) × coats × multipliers**

Same structure for ceiling sqft and trim lnft.

### Door formula

> **Door hours = door count × hrs/door (by scope)**  
> Then multiply by prep/access complexity.

## 6) Worked examples (so your “instinct” calibrates)

### Example 1 — Typical bedroom (mid estimate)

- Bedroom 12×12, 8’ ceilings
    
- Walls: ~384 sqft (40 lf perimeter × 8’)
    
- Ceiling: 144 sqft
    
- Trim: ~40 lf base + (1 door casing ~14 lf) + (1 window ~12 lf) ≈ 66 lnft
    
- 1 door, both sides
    
- Prep B (medium patches), lightly furnished
    

**Rates (mid):**

- Walls: 75 sqft/hr/coat
    
- Ceiling: 85 sqft/hr/coat
    
- Trim: 75 lnft/hr/coat
    
- Door both sides: 2.25 hrs/door (mid)
    

**Coats:**

- Walls 2 coats
    
- Ceiling 1 coat
    
- Trim 2 coats
    
- Door 2 coats
    

**Compute hours (before multipliers):**

- Walls: (384/75)*2 ≈ 10.24 hrs
    
- Ceiling: (144/85)*1 ≈ 1.69 hrs
    
- Trim: (66/75)*2 ≈ 1.76 hrs
    
- Door: 2.25 hrs  
    Subtotal ≈ **15.94 hrs**
    

**Multipliers / adders:**

- Prep B: 1.2x
    
- Lightly furnished protection: +0.75 hr
    

Total ≈ (15.94 × 1.2) + 0.75 ≈ **19.88 hrs**

If productive hours/day = 6.0:  
Painter-days ≈ 19.88 / 6.0 = **3.31 painter-days**

- 2 painters → **~1.7 calendar days**
    
- 3 painters → **~1.1 calendar days**
    

That “feels right” for a bedroom with trim + door both sides + real prep.

### Example 2 — Same bedroom (high estimate / fast mode)

Vacant, Prep A, repeatable work, strong crew:

- Walls rate 105 sqft/hr/coat
    
- Ceiling 115 sqft/hr
    
- Trim 105 lnft/hr/coat
    
- Door both sides 1.75 hrs
    

You’ll land closer to **~11–13 hours** total, i.e. **~2 painter-days**, i.e. **1 day with 2 painters**.

## 7) How to get scary-accurate (the “pro move”)

The PCA/industry guidance is consistent: **track production rates by task unit**, then reuse them.

Here’s the simple log that will make you elite in 30 days:

- Job type (vacant/occupied)
    
- Prep grade (A/B/C)
    
- Walls sqft + coats + actual wall hours
    
- Ceilings sqft + coats + actual hours
    
- Trim lnft + coats + actual hours
    
- Doors count + scope + actual hours
    

After 10–15 jobs, your “mid rate” becomes _your_ mid rate, and your low/high become predictable.
